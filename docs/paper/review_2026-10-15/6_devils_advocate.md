### Strongest Counter-Argument

The paper's central story is: compressed memory loses the timeline, a video-independent temporal prior fills the gaps, and CoCo restores the timeline and contrasts the prior away. A skeptic would say most of it belongs to the authors' output format, not to the model or compression.

Every main-table run uses a "Seen: … / Answer: … / Time: [s, e]" timeline prompt. In that format a chronological list naturally begins near 0 s, and the final interval copies the first listed moment. The "temporal prior" (90% of no-video answers start in the first 5% of the window) is exactly what this format produces when the list has nothing else to say. The paper's own numbers agree:
- Random pruning does not raise the leak: all tokens leak 37–41%, against 31–38% at random 10%.
- With the official direct-answer prompt, PCD gives +0.4 mIoU.
- A different listing format alone gains +7.4*.

The post-draft results go further. On the same QVHighlights subset with the same model, Coverage scores 30.7 mIoU under the timeline prompt and 61.7 under a one-line prompt. Timeline + PCD is 17.8 points (significant) below the one-line prompt. Unpruned Qwen3 under the one-line prompt reaches about 60 mIoU, and PCD adds nothing there (−0.1, −0.3).

"CoCo nearly doubles the uncompressed model" therefore means that CoCo partly repairs a handicapped prompt. Part of that repair is the answer reserve, which fixes run-ons that PCD itself causes (+6.1* on QVH).

What survives is narrower. An equal per-frame quota filled by question attention modestly beats random selection on long, sparsely sampled videos (+2.0* to +3.5* at 31%; +6.1* to +6.8* under the timeline prompt). It does not help on short videos. It is not evidence for a model-level temporal prior, and it does not support a near-doubling over uncompressed inference.

### Ignored Alternative Explanations/Paths
1. **Format anchoring rather than a model prior.** A chronological `Seen:` list naturally starts at the earliest listed moment, and the interval copies it. Under weak evidence the list starts at 0 s. This explains the no-video signature, why the leak does not rise with random pruning, why one instruction fails while a format change works (+7.4*), and why PCD is null under one-line prompts. It is simpler than a video-independent decoder prior and fits all the reported data.
2. **"Change the prompt" as a zero-cost baseline that dominates.** Under the one-line prompt, unpruned and Coverage-pruned Qwen3 score about 58–62 mIoU on a QVH subset with no PCD. A practitioner would pick this over CoCo-with-timeline. The paper never compares the two at main-table level.
3. **Question attention, not coverage, drives the memory-side gain.** Stratified HERMES (equal quota) stays below random. Cover ≈ Random on short videos. The missing "question global top-k" row may show most of the gain without the quota.
4. **PCD as calibrated lengthening.** On three of four pooled datasets, CoCo ties a single length-rescaling factor. Where PCD beats rescaling (QVH), the timeline prompt's failure mode and the reserve offer competing explanations.
5. **KV access as the operative advantage.** CoCo sees full-memory attention at every layer. The baselines do not. Any attention-informed KV eviction (SnapKV-style) may recover most of the gain.

### Missing Stakeholder Perspectives
- **Deployers who need prefill savings.** CoCo's memory is built from a full uncompressed prefill, so time-to-first-token and peak memory are unchanged. The "visual-token budget" framing invites readers to expect savings the method does not deliver. A cost table is TODO.
- **Users of grounding-tuned models (TimeLens, VideoMind-style) with native prompts.** For them PCD is a no-op, and only a modest Coverage gain remains. The paper does not address this audience.
- **Benchmark maintainers.** The floor analysis is useful to them, but the paper's own use of test-tuned subsets (closed intervals) and per-dataset budgets shows the same selective-evaluation risk it warns about.

### Unexamined Premise
The paper assumes the timeline prompt is a neutral measurement instrument shared by all methods, so that comparisons inside it carry over to the task. Every main-table number is conditioned on that prompt. The post-draft data show that the prompt itself changes absolute performance by tens of points, and that it determines whether PCD works at all. Sharing a prompt across methods controls for the prompt only if the method-by-prompt interaction is negligible. Here that interaction is the largest effect in the study.

### Observations (Non-Defects)
- **Post-draft results that favour the paper.** TimeLens-8B on S-EMBER (timeline prompt, 10%) reproduces the ordering random (28.2) < unpruned (33.1) < R + Coverage (37.2) < Random + PCD (38.4) < CoCo (43.9 mIoU). This partly answers the single-model concern for the timeline-prompt setting (answers not yet judged; unpruned at bs 1 vs pruned at bs 8).
- Coverage beats Random under the native prompt on the QVH subset (+3.5* Qwen3, +2.0* TimeLens-8B at 31%), and on NExT-GQA answering (+3.1* Acc@QA at 10%, both models). The memory-side contribution is real in some form; its size and mechanism are what is contested.
- QA pruners falling below random on grounding is consistent across S-EMBER, QVH and Charades, and SemVID is again the worst at 30% on NExT-GQA (−1.8* vs Cover). This finding is robust and useful.
- The paper reports floors, length rescaling, a negative-results table and the datasets where it fails. That transparency is unusual, and it made this stress test possible.
- The reproduction checks of the TimeLens-Bench scripts against published numbers (48.5/59.6/47.1 vs 48.3/59.4/46.8) suggest the evaluation pipeline is sound. That makes the prompt gap in C1 more, not less, credible.
