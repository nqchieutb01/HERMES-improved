\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Ask a video assistant ``how long did I use the paper cutter?'' and a useful answer has two parts: a duration and
the interval it comes from. Such \emph{grounded} questions are central to egocentric assistants, video search and
step-by-step guidance. They are hard for the same reason they are useful: the model must know \emph{when} things
happened across the whole window, including the moments just before and after the event.

At the same time, video LLMs pay for every frame. Qwen3-VL~\cite{qwen3vl} spends about 330 visual tokens per pair
of frames, so 64 frames already cost about 21{,}000 tokens, and long or streaming video multiplies this. Pruning
the visual tokens to a small budget is therefore the default, and a fast-growing literature reports that
10--25\% of the tokens preserve multiple-choice accuracy~\cite{fastvid,vidcom2,flashvid,semvid,prunevid,dycoke}.
This paper asks what pruning does to \emph{temporal grounding}, and finds that the answer differs from QA in three
ways.

\paragraph{Observation 1: QA pruners lose the timeline.}
Video LLMs such as Qwen3-VL report time by reading timestamp tokens interleaved with the visual tokens. A frame
whose visual tokens are all removed loses its timestamp, or keeps a timestamp with nothing to show. Pruners
designed for QA remove frames that look redundant, unremarkable or unrelated to the words of the question, and
those frames are exactly what makes a timeline usable for grounding. On S-EMBER grounded QA, all four recent
pruners we evaluate fall below \emph{random} token selection at the same budget, and the same holds on
QVHighlights and Charades-STA (\cref{sec:pruners}). Random selection is a strong baseline for grounding because it
keeps a few tokens on every frame.

\paragraph{Observation 2: where the memory is silent, a prior answers.}
Asked with no video at all, the model still produces confident intervals, and 90\% of them start in the first 5\%
of the window. A pruned memory shows the same habit: 30--56\% of predicted intervals start in the first 5\% of the
window, against 16\% of the gold intervals, and removing the frames that contain the evidence raises this share
from 37\% to 51\%. The end of the interval suffers most: the median end error is two to three times the start
error, and without video 87\% of predicted ends fall more than half an interval too early (\cref{sec:prior}).

\paragraph{Observation 3: benchmark floors are high.}
Grounding benchmarks have strong positional regularities~\cite{otani2020uncovering}. On S-EMBER, where questions are
asked mid-stream and two thirds of the gold intervals end within the last fifth of the window, the best
\emph{constant} interval $[0.15q, q]$ reaches 36.9 mIoU, more than the uncompressed model (28.0). On ActivityNet
Captions and Charades-STA the best constant also beats the uncompressed model. A claimed gain in grounding under
compression must clear these floors, and we report them for every method (\cref{sec:floors}).

\medskip
These observations suggest that a compressed memory for grounding needs two things that QA-oriented pruning does
not provide: \textbf{coverage}, so that every part of the timeline keeps an anchor in memory, and a way to stop the
decoder's \textbf{prior} from writing the interval where the evidence is thin. We propose \method (Coverage $+$
PCD), a training-free method with one component for each (\cref{fig:overview}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Coverage} (\cref{sec:coverage}). After the frames are encoded, the KV cache is pruned layer by
  layer. Every frame keeps an equal share of the layer's budget, and within each frame the tokens that the
  \emph{actual question} attends to most are kept. Because a timestamp survives exactly when one of its frame's
  visual tokens survives, the equal quota keeps every time anchor, and the question decides what each anchor shows.
  \item \textbf{Prior-contrastive decoding (PCD)} (\cref{sec:contrast}). While the answer is written, each time value is chosen by
  contrasting the model's prediction with its prediction from a video-free memory. PCD acts only on time
  values, and its strength fades where the model is already confident. An \emph{answer reserve}
  (\cref{sec:reserve}) guarantees that the final interval is always written.
\end{itemize}

Neither operator is new in isolation: per-frame budgets appear in VidCom\textsuperscript{2}, KTV and
AdaTP~\cite{vidcom2,ktv,adatp}, question-attention KV eviction in SnapKV and PruneVid~\cite{snapkv,prunevid}, and
contrast against a degraded input in VCD and M3ID~\cite{vcd,m3id}. Our contributions are to show \emph{why}
temporal grounding needs this particular combination, and to make it work:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A diagnosis of grounding under compression} (\cref{sec:diagnosis}). QA-oriented pruners lose
  temporal coverage and fall below random selection; the decoder's video-free prior fills the gaps, mostly at the
  interval end; and benchmark floors are high enough to hide both effects.
  \item \textbf{\method} (\cref{sec:method}), which couples time-anchor coverage with prior-contrastive decoding of
  time values. It needs no training, no labelled calibration and no change to the model.
  \item \textbf{A floor-adjusted evaluation} (\cref{sec:setup}) that reports, for every method, the best
  video-free constant interval, a length-rescaling baseline, and closed versus open intervals, with paired
  confidence intervals. All design choices were made on S-EMBER; the other three datasets were run once with the
  frozen configuration.
  \item \textbf{Results on four datasets} (\cref{sec:results}). With 10\% of the tokens, \method exceeds the
  uncompressed model and every pruner on S-EMBER and QVHighlights, and clears the video-free floors by
  \key{$+7.6$} mIoU on S-EMBER closed intervals (appendix) and \key{$+22.1$} on QVHighlights, both significant. Question-driven
  selection improves as the budget shrinks, as long as each frame keeps enough tokens; at very small budgets, fewer
  frames with more tokens each recover what 64 frames lose. We also report the two datasets where the base model
  itself stays below the floor, and a broad set of negative results.
\end{enumerate}
