\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Ask a video assistant ``how long did I use the paper cutter?'' and a useful answer has two parts, a duration and
the interval it comes from. Such \emph{grounded} questions are central to egocentric assistants, video search and
step-by-step guidance. What makes them useful also makes them hard. The model must know when things happened across
the whole window, including the moments just before and after the event.

At the same time, video LLMs pay for every frame. Qwen3-VL~\cite{qwen3vl} spends about 330 visual tokens per frame
(660 per pair of frames), so 64 frames already cost about 21{,}000 tokens, and long or streaming video multiplies this.
Pruning the visual tokens to a small budget has therefore become the default, and a fast-growing literature reports
that 10--25\% of the tokens are enough to preserve multiple-choice accuracy~\cite{fastvid,vidcom2,flashvid,semvid,prunevid,dycoke}.
In this paper we ask what pruning does to \emph{temporal grounding}. Our answer comes down to one principle with two
consequences: \textbf{pruning for QA keeps what is salient, but grounding needs what is everywhere.} A QA answer can
be read off a few informative frames. An interval can only be read from a timeline, and a timeline needs an anchor in
every part of the window.

\paragraph{Observation 1: QA pruners lose the anchors.}
Video LLMs such as Qwen3-VL report time by reading timestamp tokens interleaved with the visual tokens. When all
visual tokens of a frame are removed, its timestamp goes too, or it stays with nothing to show. Pruners designed for
QA drop frames that look redundant, unremarkable or unrelated to the words of the question, and those are exactly the
frames that make a timeline usable. On S-EMBER grounded QA, all four recent pruners we evaluate fall below random
token selection at the same budget, and the same holds on QVHighlights and Charades-STA (\cref{sec:pruners}). Random
selection is a strong grounding baseline because it leaves a few tokens on every frame. In fact, one of the published
scores improves by $5.3^*$ mIoU just by ranking tokens within each frame instead of globally
(\cref{sec:coverage-loss}).

\paragraph{Observation 2: where the memory is silent, a prior answers.}
Grounded QA invites a reasoning-style answer in which the model lists what it saw and when, and then states the
interval. If we ask this way with no video at all, the model still produces confident intervals, and 90\% of them
start in the first 5\% of the window. Pruned memories show the same habit: 30--56\% of their predicted intervals
start in the first 5\% of the window, compared with 16\% of the gold intervals, and removing the frames that contain
the evidence raises this share from 37\% to 51\% (\cref{sec:prior}). So the prior does not belong to any particular
pruner. It is what the decoder writes when the memory has nothing to say.

\paragraph{Observation 3: the answer format decides which failure dominates.}
These two observations call for two remedies, and the remedies do not matter equally everywhere. On the same
QVHighlights queries and with the same memory, asking for a timeline of observations instead of a single interval
costs about 30 mIoU (\cref{sec:transfer}). With the one-line format, selecting tokens by the question helps and
contrastive decoding does nothing. With the timeline format, the video-free prior writes many of the times, so
contrasting it away helps, as long as the contrast does not break the answer format. For this reason we treat the
answer format as an experimental variable instead of a fixed detail of the protocol.

\paragraph{Observation 4: benchmark floors are high.}
Grounding benchmarks have strong positional regularities~\cite{otani2020uncovering}. On S-EMBER, questions are asked
mid-stream and two thirds of the gold intervals end within the last fifth of the window. As a result, the best
\emph{constant} interval $[0.15q, q]$ reaches 36.9 mIoU, which is more than the uncompressed model gets when it answers
in the timeline format (28.0). Following \citet{otani2020uncovering}, we report video-free floors for every method
(\cref{sec:floors}).

\medskip
We propose \method (BOLT $+$ Coverage $+$ Contrastive decoding), a training-free method with one component for each
failure. An optional BOLT front end chooses which frames to encode (\cref{fig:overview}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Coverage} (\cref{sec:coverage}) keeps the anchors. Once the frames are encoded, we prune the KV cache
  layer by layer. Each frame gets an equal share of the layer's budget, and within each frame we keep the tokens that
  the actual question attends to most. A timestamp survives exactly when one of its frame's visual tokens survives,
  so the equal quota guarantees every time anchor while the question decides what each anchor shows. In the settings
  we tested, a global top-$k$ over the same question attention does as well without the quota. The question score is
  therefore what drives the gain, and the quota acts as a safeguard for cases where scores collapse onto a few frames.
  \item \textbf{Prior-contrastive decoding (PCD)} (\cref{sec:contrast}) removes the prior where it writes the answer.
  We choose each time value by contrasting the model's prediction with its prediction from a video-free memory. This
  is the content-free contrast used in contextual calibration and context-aware decoding~\cite{calibratebeforeuse,cad},
  applied only to time values and with a strength that fades where the model is already confident.
\end{itemize}

Neither operator is new on its own. Per-frame budgets appear in VidCom\textsuperscript{2}, KTV and
AdaTP~\cite{vidcom2,ktv,adatp}, question-attention KV eviction in SnapKV and PruneVid~\cite{snapkv,prunevid}, and
contrast against a content-free or degraded input in contrastive decoding, classifier-free guidance and
VCD~\cite{contrastivedecoding,cfglm,cad,vcd,m3id}. What we contribute is an account of \emph{why} temporal grounding
needs each of them and when each one matters, together with a way to make them work together:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A diagnosis of grounding under compression} (\cref{sec:diagnosis}). QA-oriented pruners empty frames
  and fall below random selection. In reasoning-style answers the decoder's video-free prior fills the gaps, and an
  evidence-removal test shows this causally.
  \item \textbf{Coverage}, per-layer question-driven token selection under an equal quota (\cref{sec:coverage}).
  With the standard one-line prompt, on the full Charades-STA and QVHighlights sets and on both Qwen3-VL-8B and the
  grounding-tuned TimeLens-8B, it matches the uncompressed model with 24\% of the visual tokens. It also beats random
  selection ($+0.5^*$ to $+1.6^*$ mIoU) and a matched low-resolution input ($+2.1^*$ to $+2.6^*$)
  (\cref{sec:transfer}). On NExT-GQA at 10\% of the tokens it keeps the accuracy of the uncompressed model, while random
  selection loses about four points ($+3.4^*$ and $+3.2^*$ over random). With BOLT frames it exceeds a 1\,fps input
  of similar budget by $+9.2^*$ and $+6.5^*$ mIoU. Random tokens on the same frames, however, already gain $+7.0^*$
  and $+6.1^*$, so most of that gain comes from the front end.
  \item \textbf{PCD and an answer-format study} (\cref{sec:contrast,sec:transfer}). On S-EMBER, PCD helps
  reasoning-style answers on all eight memories we test and on both models. With 10\% of the tokens, \method improves
  on the uncompressed model by $+11.2^*$ and $+10.7^*$ mIoU under the same prompt. The same study shows that the
  answer format alone moves single-moment grounding by about 30 mIoU and decides whether contrastive decoding helps.
  PCD does nothing for one-line answers, and on NExT-GQA it lowers the scored grounding where it disturbs the format
  (TimeLens-8B timeline answers, minutes:seconds under the official prompt).
  \item \textbf{Floor-adjusted, reproducible evaluation} (\cref{sec:setup}). We give video-free floors and a
  length-rescaling baseline for every method, paired bootstrap tests, a reproduction of the published TimeLens-Bench
  numbers within 0.3 mIoU, and sanity checks with no video and with shuffled frames or timestamps. We also report a
  broad set of negative results, including the limits of Coverage on short, densely sampled videos and of PCD outside
  the timeline format.
\end{enumerate}
