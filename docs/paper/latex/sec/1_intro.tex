\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Ask a video assistant ``how long did I use the paper cutter?'' and a useful answer has two parts: a duration and
the interval it comes from. Such \emph{grounded} questions are central to egocentric assistants, video search and
step-by-step guidance. They are hard for the same reason they are useful: the model must know \emph{when} things
happened across the whole window, including the moments just before and after the event.

At the same time, video LLMs pay for every frame. Qwen3-VL~\cite{qwen3vl} spends about 330 visual tokens per frame
(660 per pair of frames), so 64 frames already cost about 21{,}000 tokens, and long or streaming video multiplies this.
Pruning the visual tokens to a small budget is therefore the default, and a fast-growing literature reports that
10--25\% of the tokens preserve multiple-choice accuracy~\cite{fastvid,vidcom2,flashvid,semvid,prunevid,dycoke}.
This paper asks what pruning does to \emph{temporal grounding}. Our answer is a single principle with two
consequences: \textbf{pruning for QA keeps what is salient, but grounding needs what is everywhere.} A QA answer can
be read from a few informative frames; an interval can only be read from a timeline, and a timeline needs an anchor
in every part of the window.

\paragraph{Observation 1: QA pruners lose the anchors.}
Video LLMs such as Qwen3-VL report time by reading timestamp tokens interleaved with the visual tokens. A frame
whose visual tokens are all removed loses its timestamp, or keeps a timestamp with nothing to show. Pruners
designed for QA remove frames that look redundant, unremarkable or unrelated to the words of the question, and
those frames are exactly what makes a timeline usable. On S-EMBER grounded QA, all four recent pruners we evaluate
fall below \emph{random} token selection at the same budget, and the same holds on QVHighlights and Charades-STA
(\cref{sec:pruners}). Random selection is a strong baseline for grounding because it keeps a few tokens on every
frame, and one of the published scores improves by $5.3^*$ mIoU simply by being ranked within each frame instead of
globally (\cref{sec:coverage-loss}).

\paragraph{Observation 2: where the memory is silent, a prior answers.}
Grounded QA invites a reasoning-style answer: the model lists what it saw and when, then states the interval. Asked
this way with no video at all, the model still produces confident intervals, and 90\% of them start in the first
5\% of the window. A pruned memory shows the same habit: 30--56\% of predicted intervals start in the first 5\% of
the window, against 16\% of the gold intervals, and removing the frames that contain the evidence raises this share
from 37\% to 51\% (\cref{sec:prior}). The prior is therefore not a property of a particular pruner; it is what the
decoder writes where the memory has nothing to say.

\paragraph{Observation 3: the answer format decides which failure dominates.}
The two observations call for two remedies, and they do not matter equally everywhere. On the same QVHighlights
queries and the same memory, asking for a timeline of observations instead of a single interval costs about 30 mIoU
(\cref{sec:transfer}). Under the one-line format, selecting tokens by the question is what helps and contrastive
decoding does nothing; under the timeline format, the video-free prior writes many of the times, and contrasting it
away is what helps, as long as the contrast does not break the answer format. We therefore treat the answer format as an experimental variable, not a fixed detail of the protocol.

\paragraph{Observation 4: benchmark floors are high.}
Grounding benchmarks have strong positional regularities~\cite{otani2020uncovering}. On S-EMBER, where questions are
asked mid-stream and two thirds of the gold intervals end within the last fifth of the window, the best
\emph{constant} interval $[0.15q, q]$ reaches 36.9 mIoU, more than the uncompressed model answering in the timeline
format (28.0). Following \citet{otani2020uncovering}, we report video-free floors for every method
(\cref{sec:floors}).

\medskip
We propose \method (BOLT $+$ Coverage $+$ Contrastive decoding), a training-free method with one component for each
failure, behind an optional BOLT front end that chooses which frames to encode (\cref{fig:overview}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Coverage} (\cref{sec:coverage}) keeps the anchors. After the frames are encoded, the KV cache is
  pruned layer by layer. Every frame keeps an equal share of the layer's budget, and within each frame the tokens
  that the \emph{actual question} attends to most are kept. Because a timestamp survives exactly when one of its
  frame's visual tokens survives, the equal quota guarantees every time anchor, and the question decides what each
  anchor shows. In the settings we tested, a global top-$k$ of the same question attention without the quota does as
  well, so the question score drives the gain and the quota is a safeguard against scores that collapse onto a few
  frames.
  \item \textbf{Prior-contrastive decoding (PCD)} (\cref{sec:contrast}) removes the prior where it writes the answer.
  Each time value is chosen by contrasting the model's prediction with its prediction from a video-free memory, the
  content-free contrast of contextual calibration and context-aware decoding~\cite{calibratebeforeuse,cad} applied
  only to time values, with a strength that fades where the model is already confident.
\end{itemize}

Neither operator is new in isolation: per-frame budgets appear in VidCom\textsuperscript{2}, KTV and
AdaTP~\cite{vidcom2,ktv,adatp}, question-attention KV eviction in SnapKV and PruneVid~\cite{snapkv,prunevid}, and
contrast against a content-free or degraded input in contrastive decoding, classifier-free guidance and
VCD~\cite{contrastivedecoding,cfglm,cad,vcd,m3id}. Our contributions are to show \emph{why} temporal grounding needs
each of them, \emph{when} each one matters, and to make them work together:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A diagnosis of grounding under compression} (\cref{sec:diagnosis}). QA-oriented pruners empty frames
  and fall below random selection; in reasoning-style answers the decoder's video-free prior fills the gaps, which an
  evidence-removal test shows causally.
  \item \textbf{Coverage}, per-layer question-driven token selection under an equal quota (\cref{sec:coverage}).
  Under the standard one-line prompt, on the full Charades-STA and QVHighlights sets and on both Qwen3-VL-8B and the
  grounding-tuned TimeLens-8B, it matches the uncompressed model with 24\% of the visual tokens and beats random
  selection ($+0.5^*$ to $+1.6^*$ mIoU) and a matched low-resolution input ($+2.1^*$ to $+2.6^*$)
  (\cref{sec:transfer}). On NExT-GQA at 10\% of the tokens it keeps the accuracy of the uncompressed model, where random
  selection loses about four points ($+3.4^*$ and $+3.2^*$ over random). With BOLT frames it exceeds a 1\,fps input
  of a similar budget by $+9.2^*$ and $+6.5^*$ mIoU, but random tokens on the same frames already gain $+7.0^*$ and $+6.1^*$: that gain belongs
  mostly to the front end.
  \item \textbf{PCD and an answer-format study} (\cref{sec:contrast,sec:transfer}). On S-EMBER, PCD helps
  reasoning-style answers on all eight memories we test and on both models; with 10\% of the tokens \method improves
  on the uncompressed model by $+11.2^*$ and $+10.7^*$ mIoU under the same prompt. The same study shows that the
  answer format alone moves single-moment grounding by about 30 mIoU and decides whether contrastive decoding helps:
  PCD does nothing for one-line answers, and on NExT-GQA it lowers the scored grounding where it disturbs the format
  (TimeLens-8B timeline answers, minutes:seconds under the official prompt).
  \item \textbf{Floor-adjusted, reproducible evaluation} (\cref{sec:setup}). Video-free floors and a length-rescaling
  baseline for every method, paired bootstrap tests, a reproduction of the published TimeLens-Bench numbers within
  0.3 mIoU, sanity checks with no video and shuffled frames or timestamps, and a broad set of negative results,
  including the limits of Coverage on short, densely sampled videos and of PCD outside the timeline format.
\end{enumerate}
