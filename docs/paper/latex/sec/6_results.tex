\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We organise the results by question. \Cref{sec:main} compares \method with all baselines and floors on four datasets.
\Cref{sec:pruners} shows how published pruners behave on grounding. \Cref{sec:res-coverage,sec:res-contrast} analyse
the two components. \Cref{sec:budget,sec:frames} study the budget and how to spend it. \Cref{sec:oracles} compares
with upper bounds that use the gold interval, \cref{sec:res-retrieval} analyses the optional retrieval stage,
\cref{sec:negative} lists what did not work, and \cref{sec:errors} analyses the remaining errors.

\paragraph{Reading the tables.}
Numbers are mIoU, or mIoU\,/\,R@0.5 where two are given. $\Delta$ is a paired difference; $^*$ marks differences whose 95\%
paired bootstrap interval excludes 0. Tables built from the early S-EMBER phases (\cref{tab:pcd8,tab:cf,%
tab:scope,tab:replay,tab:oracle,tab:errors} and \cref{tab:causal}) predate the answer reserve, so PCD there runs without it, and use the official parser and batch size 1;
on S-EMBER the two parsers differ by at most 0.7 mIoU and batch size changes no paired result. In every table the best
value is in \best{bold} and the second best \second{underlined}, per column unless a caption says per row; floors
and differences ($\Delta$) are not ranked.

\Cref{tab:main,tab:paired} lead to three conclusions.

\emph{(i) On the least biased benchmark, \method nearly doubles the uncompressed model.} On QVHighlights, with 10\%
of the tokens, \method reaches 54.8 R@0.5 (R@0.7: 30.2) against 28.6 (18.4) for all tokens and 25.0 (16.2) for random
tokens, and clears every floor by a wide margin ($+22.1$ mIoU over the best constant, $+8.6$ over length rescaling).

\emph{(ii) On S-EMBER, the gain is real where the window end is not a shortcut.} \method beats all tokens, random
selection and SemVID under the same decoding. Against the floors its pooled gain is small and not significant,
because two thirds of S-EMBER intervals end at the question time. On closed intervals, where that shortcut does not
exist, \method gains $+7.6^*$ mIoU over the best constant and $+7.5^*$ over length rescaling
(\cref{tab:closed}).

\emph{(iii) On ActivityNet and Charades-STA, the base model is the bottleneck.} The uncompressed model is far below
the constant floor on both (22.9 \vs 37.9 and 16.6 \vs 29.7). \method improves on all tokens and on every pruner on
ActivityNet, but no method reaches the floor, and on Charades-STA Coverage is worse than random selection
(\cref{sec:frames}). We report these as limits, not successes.

\subsection{QA pruners on grounding}
\label{sec:pruners}

\input{fig/pruners}

Every published pruner (\cref{fig:pruners}) falls below random token selection on S-EMBER ($-2.3$ to $-7.6$ mIoU, all significant),
QVHighlights ($-2.4$ to $-7.7$; SemVID not significant) and Charades-STA ($-2.8$ to $-5.2$, all significant);
on ActivityNet at 10\% the input-level pruners are above random, which we discuss in \cref{sec:frames}. Random
selection varies little across seeds ($26.0 \pm 0.6$ mIoU over three seeds on S-EMBER), so the gaps are not noise.
The ordering follows coverage (\cref{tab:retention}): the methods that empty frames most aggressively lose most, and
those that spread tokens lose least. Accuracy, in contrast, is barely affected (\cref{tab:qa}): FastVID's accuracy
is within noise of random selection ($-2.1$, n.s.) while its mIoU is 7.6 lower. A pruner built to keep answers keeps answers, not time.
PCD helps every pruner ($+7$ to $+12$ mIoU) but does not close the gap to \method.

\paragraph{Design choices.}
\Cref{tab:design} varies one choice at a time. \emph{The quota should be equal}: five relevance-weighted variants,
from SigLIP similarity, attention mass or both, with floors of 25--50\%, are within $\pm 0.5$ mIoU of the equal quota
and none is significant (the uniform-frame SigLIP variant was our pre-registered primary test). Quotas that follow
raw attention mass are pulled by attention sinks and recency bias: a single frame received up to 227 of about 2{,}140
kept tokens. \emph{The score should be the actual question}: HERMES's generic probe questions give 23.7 even with an
equal quota, and 18.3 without. \emph{Deterministic top-$k$ beats sampling} from a coverage-floored density (31.7
\vs 30.0); with the quota in place, coverage is already guaranteed. A contrastive token score that subtracts the
attention of a generic sentence, meant to cancel sinks, does not help.

\paragraph{Per-layer selection.}
\Cref{tab:shared} compares one token set per layer with a single set shared by all layers. Sharing does not change
random selection, which rules out per-layer diversity as the reason random selection beats input-level pruners. It
costs \method $-1.5^*$ mIoU: different layers attend to different tokens, and question-driven selection benefits from
letting each choose.

\paragraph{PCD is a plug-in for any memory.}
\Cref{tab:pcd8} applies PCD to eight memories: three random budgets, the uncompressed model, HERMES and
stratified pruning, and two HERMES streaming memories. It improves mIoU significantly on all eight ($+4.6$ to $+8.0$)

smallest. With 10\% of the tokens and random selection it already beats the uncompressed model (32.6 \vs 28.0).
PCD also transfers to FastVID ($+7.6^*$ mIoU, leak $38\% \rightarrow 12\%$), the pruner that loses coverage
most.

\paragraph{The no-video counterfactual is the right one.}
\Cref{tab:cf} changes only what the memory is contrasted against. The no-video input isolates the prior and removes
it (early starts $38\% \rightarrow 19\%$). Noised frames, as in VCD, still show part of the video \emph{and} still
produce the prior, so the two runs disagree mainly about content and little of the prior is removed ($+0.5$ mIoU).
Permuted timestamps leave the prior in place as well. A one-sided rule that only penalises tokens the video argues
against is worse than PCD ($-5.6^*$ and $-4.8^*$): without video the model is about 99\% sure of ``0'', so even a
well-supported ``0'' looks like evidence against. Boosting visual attention during decoding, or stating the bias in
the instruction, leaves the prior in place.

\paragraph{Time scope and adaptive strength.}
Contrasting every token also changes words that have nothing to do with time: the answer text changed in 85\% of
questions, lists of more than eight moments rose from 12 to 56, and over-counting rose from 14\% to 34\% of counting
questions. Restricting PCD to time values keeps the grounding gain and disturbs the text less
(\cref{tab:scope}, top). PCD also risks hurting questions whose evidence really starts at 0\,s. With a constant
strength, their accuracy on the uncompressed memory falls from 21.8 to 16.7; with the adaptive strength, which fades
when the model is confident, it rises to 23.1 (\cref{tab:scope}, bottom; $+6.4^*$ \vs constant). The adaptive rule
also gives the best \emph{discrimination}, early starts on early-evidence questions minus early starts on the
others (56.4 \vs 49.6 for greedy, all-tokens memory).

\paragraph{PCD is near the limit of decoding.}
We logged both distributions at the first time value of every answer and replayed other decision rules offline

need evidence, not a better decoding rule.

\paragraph{Is PCD more than a longer interval?}
\label{sec:lengthening}

Charades-STA the two are statistically tied, so on those sets the benefit of PCD cannot be separated from a

\paragraph{Answer reserve.}
PCD rewards fine time steps that the prior finds unlikely, so it sometimes lists one continuing activity at
one-second steps until the token limit. The reserve removes every such failure and improves every PCD configuration
we tested (\cref{tab:reserve}), most where run-ons were frequent (SemVID $+$ PCD on Charades, \method on

\paragraph{Prompt format.}
PCD needs a reasoning-style answer: with the official direct-answer prompt it does not help ($+0.4$ mIoU). An

leak. \method uses the simpler timeline in all main results. \TODO{event timeline with \method.}

\cref{tab:frames} every row of a block keeps the same number of tokens and only the split between frames and tokens

%% ================================================================================================= 6.8 retrieval
\subsection{Analysis of the optional retrieval}
\label{sec:res-retrieval}

The SigLIP~2 index does find the evidence: over the seconds before each question, the AUROC of evidence seconds
against the rest is 0.663, and the top-ranked second lies in the gold interval for 63\% of questions (chance 36\%) and
41\% of location questions (chance 13.5\%). Retrieval-guided frames put 40\% of the memory inside the evidence, against
36\% for uniform frames. This helps random tokens ($+3.8^*$ mIoU) but adds only $+0.8$ to $+0.9$ (n.s.) once tokens are
chosen by the question, because both stages focus the memory on the question. Subtracting a question-free prior from
SigLIP's score does not help (AUROC 0.635--0.666), as expected for a retriever trained with a contrastive loss, whose
score already estimates pointwise mutual information~\cite{siglip2}. Re-weighting the token budget of uniform frames
by the same relevance adds $+1.3$ (n.s.): which frames are encoded matters more than how the budget is split among
fixed frames.

\Cref{tab:errors} shows that PCD works where it was aimed: intervals entirely before the gold one, the signature
of the prior, fall from the largest error type to less than a fifth of answers. The remaining failures point to
four limits. (i) \emph{Answers, not intervals}: twice as many answers are well grounded but wrong as grounded and
right; counting answers do not use the events the model itself listed, and an IoU of 0.5 still allows a two-fold
duration error. (ii) \emph{Recent events}: 81\% of the intervals that still end before the gold one have evidence in
the second half of the window, where an earlier similar event is picked; GQ@0.5 is 4.4 when the evidence lies in the
last third of the window. (iii) \emph{Sparse evidence}: with fewer than 4 of the 64 frames inside the evidence, GQ@0.5
is 1--2\% for any method, against 15\% with 16 or more; no decoding rule recovers evidence that is not in the input.
(iv) \emph{Label ambiguity}: on the 98 questions whose annotators disagree (mean pairwise IoU $<0.6$), every method
scores near zero.
