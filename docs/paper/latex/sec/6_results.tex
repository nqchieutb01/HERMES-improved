\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\Cref{sec:main} compares \method with all baselines, each dataset under the prompt that gives the methods their best
results: the reasoning-style timeline prompt on S-EMBER and the one-line prompt on QVHighlights and Charades-STA.
\Cref{sec:transfer} covers the answer format, a grounding-tuned model and NExT-GQA, and the later subsections analyse
pruners, components, budget, gold-informed bounds and remaining errors.

\paragraph{Reading the tables.}
Numbers are mIoU, or mIoU\,/\,R@0.5 where two are given, and $\Delta$ is a paired difference. Tables from the early
S-EMBER phases (\cref{tab:pcd8,tab:cf,%
tab:scope,tab:replay,tab:errors} and \cref{tab:causal}) use an earlier implementation of PCD, the official parser and
batch size 1. On the S-EMBER rows we checked, the two parsers differ by 0.2--0.7 mIoU, except for the oracle selector
with PCD (1.5 mIoU, because 17 answers are written in minutes:seconds). All other S-EMBER tables use the
minutes:seconds-aware parser. The best value is in \best{bold} and the second best \second{underlined}, per column
unless a caption says per row. $\Delta$ columns are not ranked.

\Cref{tab:main} supports three conclusions.

(i) QA pruners lose to random selection, and Coverage does not. On S-EMBER all four published pruners score below
random tokens (\cref{sec:pruners}), while Coverage on uniform frames scores 31.7 mIoU against 25.6 for random tokens
and 28.0 for all tokens. On QVHighlights and Charades-STA the pruners fall below random selection under the timeline
prompt (\cref{fig:pruners}). With the one-line prompt on the full QVHighlights set and BOLT frames at about the same
visual budget (bottom block), FastVID and SemVID score 36.1 and 53.1 mIoU, against 55.0 for random tokens and 57.2 for
Coverage.

(ii) With the one-line prompt and about 24\% of the visual tokens, Coverage keeps the grounding of the uncompressed
model on both full sets. It leads random selection by a small margin ($+1.6$ and $+0.5$ mIoU), largest on
QVHighlights, whose videos are longer. With BOLT frames on QVHighlights (bottom block), it leads random tokens by
$+2.2$ mIoU at 1{,}192 kept tokens and $+0.6$ at 4{,}455, where it is 2.5 mIoU below and 0.9 above the uncompressed
model, respectively (\cref{sec:transfer}).

another 6.8. \method is $+11.2$ mIoU above the uncompressed model in the same format and $+4.2$ above random tokens
with PCD (\cref{tab:qa}), so the gain does not come from PCD alone. Under the one-line prompt PCD has no effect
(\cref{tab:prompt}), and the one-line \method cells are not yet run. Timeline-prompt results on 500-query subsets of
the other datasets are in \cref{tab:ladder,tab:budget,tab:frames}. On single-moment grounding that format costs the
uncompressed model about 30 mIoU.

\subsection{QA pruners on grounding}
\label{sec:pruners}

\input{fig/pruners}

Every published pruner (\cref{fig:pruners}) falls below random token selection on three of the four datasets, by
$-2.3$ to $-7.6$ mIoU on S-EMBER, $-2.4$ to $-7.7$ on QVHighlights and $-2.8$ to $-5.2$ on Charades-STA. The exception is ActivityNet at 10\%, where the
input-level pruners are above random (\cref{sec:frames}). The gaps exceed seed noise (random selection gives
$26.0 \pm 0.6$ mIoU over three seeds on S-EMBER), and their ordering follows coverage (\cref{tab:retention}). Pruners
that empty frames most aggressively lose the most, and those that spread tokens the least. Accuracy barely moves.
FastVID is 2.1 points below random selection in accuracy, about our decoding noise, but 7.6 lower in mIoU. These
pruners preserve answers, as designed, but discard the timing. PCD helps every pruner ($+7$ to $+12$ mIoU) but does
not close the gap to \method.

\paragraph{Design choices.}
\Cref{tab:design} varies one choice at a time. Given a quota, an equal one works as well as any weighted one. Five
relevance-weighted variants (SigLIP similarity, attention mass or both) are all within $\pm 0.5$ mIoU of it, including
our pre-registered primary test, the uniform-frame SigLIP variant. Quotas that follow raw attention mass are pulled by
attention sinks and recency bias, so that one frame received up to 227 of about 2{,}140 kept tokens. The score itself
should come from the actual question. HERMES's generic probe questions give 23.7 even with an equal quota, and 18.3 without one.
Deterministic top-$k$ beats sampling from a coverage-floored density (31.7 \vs 30.0), and a contrastive token score
that subtracts a generic sentence's attention does not help.

\paragraph{Is the quota needed?}
For the generic HERMES score the equal quota is worth $+5.3$ mIoU, because that score concentrates on the end of the
window and empties earlier frames (\cref{tab:retention}). With the actual question's attention, a global top-$k$ of
the same per-layer attention with no quota matched the equal quota in both settings we tested ($-0.1$ mIoU on S-EMBER
under the official prompt, \cref{tab:official}, and $-0.0$ on the 584 QVHighlights queries run so far with BOLT
frames and the one-line prompt, where it is $+1.8$ above random tokens).
There, the gain over random selection comes from scoring tokens by the question, and the equal quota guarantees that
every anchor survives, which matters for scores that collapse onto a few frames.

\paragraph{Choice of counterfactual.}
\Cref{tab:cf} changes only what the memory is contrasted against. The no-video input works best because it isolates
the prior, so contrasting against it removes the prior (early starts $38\% \rightarrow 19\%$). Noised frames, as in
VCD, still show part of the video \emph{and} still produce the prior, so the two runs disagree mainly about content
($+0.5$ mIoU). Permuted timestamps also leave the prior in place. A one-sided rule that only penalises tokens the
video argues against is worse than PCD ($-5.6$ and $-4.8$), because without video the model is about 99\% sure of
``0'' and even a well-supported ``0'' looks like evidence against. Boosting visual attention, or stating the bias in
the instruction, does not remove the prior either.

\paragraph{Time scope and adaptive strength.}
Contrasting every token also changes words unrelated to time. The answer text changed in 85\% of questions, and
over-counting rose from 14\% to 34\% of counting questions. Restricting PCD to time values keeps the grounding gain
and disturbs the text less (\cref{tab:scope}, top). A constant strength also hurts questions whose evidence really
starts at 0\,s, lowering their accuracy on the uncompressed memory from 21.8 to 16.7. The adaptive strength fades when
the model is confident and raises it to 23.1 instead (\cref{tab:scope}, bottom). It also gives the best
\emph{discrimination} (early starts on early-evidence questions minus those on the others), 56.4 against 49.6 for
greedy decoding on the all-tokens memory.

The collapse at small budgets comes from how the budget is spent, and the equal quota is not at fault in itself. Each
block of \cref{tab:frames} keeps the token count fixed and changes only the split between frames and tokens per frame
(the timestamp caveat of \cref{sec:budget} applies, since fewer frames also means fewer timestamps in the budget).

%% ================================================================================================= 6.8 retrieval
\subsection{Analysis of BOLT}
\label{sec:res-retrieval}

The SigLIP~2 index does find the evidence. Over the seconds before each question, evidence seconds reach an AUROC of
0.663 against the rest, and the top-ranked second lies in the gold interval for 63\% of questions (chance 36\%) and
41\% of location questions (chance 13.5\%). BOLT frames put 40\% of the memory inside the evidence, against 36\% for
uniform frames, which helps random tokens ($+3.8$ mIoU). Once tokens are chosen by the question, BOLT adds only $+0.8$
to $+0.9$, because both stages focus the memory on the question. Subtracting a question-free prior from SigLIP's score
does not help (AUROC 0.635--0.666), as expected for a contrastively trained retriever, whose score already estimates
pointwise mutual information~\cite{siglip2}. Re-weighting the token budget of uniform frames by the same relevance
adds $+1.3$. The choice of encoded frames matters more than how the budget is split among fixed frames.

\Cref{tab:errors} shows that PCD works where it was aimed. Intervals entirely before the gold one, the signature of
the prior, are the largest error type under greedy decoding, and PCD cuts them to less than a fifth of answers. The
remaining failures point to four limits.
(i) Wrong answers on good intervals. Twice as many answers are well grounded but wrong as grounded and right.
Counting answers ignore the events the model itself listed, and an IoU of 0.5 still allows a two-fold duration error.
(ii) Recent events. Of the intervals that still end before the gold one, 81\% have evidence in the second half of the
window, and the model picks an earlier similar event instead. With evidence in the last third, GQ@0.5 is 4.4.
(iii) Sparse evidence. With fewer than 4 of the 64 frames inside the evidence, GQ@0.5 is 1--2\% for any method,
against 15\% with 16 or more. No decoding rule can recover evidence missing from the input.
(iv) Label ambiguity. On the 98 questions whose annotators disagree (mean pairwise IoU $<0.6$), every method
scores near zero.
